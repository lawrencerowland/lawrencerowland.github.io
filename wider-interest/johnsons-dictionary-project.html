Literature · work · uncertainty

Johnson’s Dictionary

A three-year promise became a nine-year undertaking. What can a modern project lens reveal about the work—and where does that lens mislead?

Johnson signed a contract with London booksellers on 18 June 1746, for 1,500 guineas, with a three-year ambition. The Dictionary appeared on 15 April 1755. He did not work entirely alone: assistants copied the quotations he selected from books. His wife Elizabeth “Tetty” Johnson died in 1752. Sources and reading ↓

The published Dictionary filled two folio volumes and contained roughly 43,000 entries. Each entry could bring together several meanings and literary examples: the scale lay in those editorial decisions as well as the word count.

Two kinds of evidence: the dated milestones below are historical. The workstream bars and risk matrix are modern teaching interpretations; no surviving project schedule or contemporary probability assessment is claimed.

What the historical example can—and cannot—teach

Estimation: the contract-to-publication gap is documented. Calling it “scope creep” would need evidence about what was agreed and subsequently changed. Quotations were part of the dictionary’s design; their presence alone does not prove uncontrolled expansion.

Collaboration: assistants’ copying and organising work matters, even where Johnson retained editorial responsibility. “One man did it all” hides that work.

Resources and scope: a modern team would need a budget for research, assistants, materials and printing, alongside an agreement about which costs the author or publisher pays. A longer schedule can strain that budget even when the intended scope stays the same. The team would also need to decide which words, meanings and examples belong in the work, and how proposed additions will be assessed. These are planning questions for a present-day analogue, not evidence of particular overruns in Johnson’s project.

Human circumstances: bereavement belongs in an account of a life. A death is not evidence that it was a “high-probability risk”, and a retrospective matrix cannot recover contemporary expectations.
